Anatomy Lab Study Resources

Supplemental study materials designed to help students retain anatomy lab content beyond the session, with self-paced practice they can return to between labs.

  • Challenge Students struggled to retain anatomy lab content beyond the session.
  • Approach Designed supplemental study resources that chunk content to manage cognitive load, pair visuals with text (dual coding), and scaffold from guided to independent practice.
  • Result Students gained a flexible, self-paced way to review and reinforce lab learning between sessions.

Intended learning outcomes

1. Discuss joint kinematics and muscle contraction in the analysis of human movement.

  • 1.1Describe movements of the body using correct biomechanical and anatomical terminology.
  • 1.2Identify muscle recruitment patterns for all major joint actions of the body.
  • 1.3Identify muscle contraction types during the phases of repetitive strength training exercises.

How I organized the content and built learner engagement

Anatomy lab content is dense and full of unfamiliar terminology, so I designed the resources to reduce the mental effort of getting started and to build confidence step by step.

  • Chunking. I broke the content into short, focused sections organized by joint and movement, so students could study one idea at a time instead of facing the whole lab at once. This manages cognitive load and makes it easier to review a single topic.
  • Dual coding. I paired diagrams and labelled visuals with concise text, so students could connect terminology to what a movement or muscle looks like. Combining images and words gives them two routes to remember the same idea.
  • Scaffolded practice. Activities move from guided support, such as labelled examples and prompts, toward independent recall, where students identify movements, muscles and contraction types on their own. The support fades as confidence grows.
  • Retrieval practice and self-checking. Low-stakes practice questions let students test themselves, spot gaps and revisit weak areas, which strengthens long-term retention more than rereading notes.
  • Applying the terminology. Practice tied each concept to real exercise movements, such as the phases of a strength training exercise, so the vocabulary connected to meaningful examples.
  • Plain structure and clear signposting. Consistent layouts, headings and short instructions helped students always know what to do next and how the material linked to the outcomes.

How I checked accessibility and alignment with the outcomes

Alignment with the intended outcomes. I mapped every section and practice activity back to a specific learning outcome (1.1, 1.2 or 1.3) and removed or reworked anything that did not support one. I also checked that the practice asked students to do what the outcomes require, such as describing, identifying and discussing, so the resources prepared them for how they would be assessed.

Accessibility. I reviewed the resources against accessibility best practice, including:

  • Clear heading structure and consistent layout, so the content is easy to scan and navigate
  • Alternative text or text descriptions for diagrams and images that carry meaning
  • Sufficient colour contrast, and not relying on colour alone to convey information
  • Readable font sizes and plain language, with key terms defined where they first appear
  • Meaningful link text and a format that works with screen readers and on different devices
